There's a romantic idea that the best images happen by accident on set. Occasionally they do. But the reason those happy accidents get noticed, captured and used is that everything else on the day was planned to the minute.

## Write the shot list backwards

We start with where the work will live. A billboard, a product page, a nine-second vertical video and a press image all want different things from the same moment. If you know that before the shoot, you can frame one setup to serve several placements. If you don't, you discover it in the edit — when it's too late and too expensive to fix.

So the shot list begins with the deliverables, then works back to scenes, setups and finally the schedule.

## Every shot needs a reason

Each line on the list should answer a simple question: what is this image for? "Hero, wide, for the homepage and outdoor" is useful. "Nice moody one" is not. The reason keeps a crew focused at 4pm when the light is going and everyone is tired.

## Leave room for the good accident

Planning is not the enemy of spontaneity — it's what pays for it. When the essentials are captured by lunchtime, the afternoon is free to chase the frame nobody planned. That's usually where the best picture is.

## The quiet benefit

A clear shot list is also the easiest way to keep a client confident. They can see what will be made, why and when. Nobody spends the day wondering whether the thing they need is going to exist.

Good production looks effortless. That's because the effort happened weeks earlier, on paper.
